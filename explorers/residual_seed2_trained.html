1. Choose what counts as a match

A pair matches when its distance is at or below the cutoff. Smaller = stricter; larger = more permissive. These are distances, not similarity percentages. Changes apply immediately.

Explain all three comparisons
Signed shape
Divide each kernel by its magnitude, then compare the patterns. A kernel and twice that kernel have the same signed shape. Flipped signs remain different.
Original weight values
Compare the nine original values. The distance is their Euclidean difference divided by the smaller kernel magnitude, so magnitude differences matter.
Centered shape
Subtract each kernel's own mean, then normalize and compare. This asks whether the pattern remains similar after removing its average level.

2. Browse kernels that have partners

These buttons skip kernels with no partners under the current comparison and cutoff. They browse the entire snapshot in kernel-ID order, wrapping around at either end. They do not rank matches by strength.

Or select a specific kernel by ID or location

Use either ID or coordinates; you do not need to fill in both. For example, B01.conv1 / output 5 / input 12 selects the kernel connecting those channels in block 1's first convolution. Stem is the first convolution; conv1 and conv2 are the two convolutions within each block.

Why can a partner be in a different tight group?

A direct partner matches your selected kernel. A tight group requires every pair within it to match. If A matches B and B matches C, but A does not match C, all three cannot form one tight group. The table includes every direct partner, even if the clustering assigns it to another group.

Group size includes the selected kernel. A singleton has group size 1 and may still have a direct partner in another group. Group colors are visual aids, not identities across models or cutoffs.

Inside this kernel: its nine original weights

This grid shows spatial positions: top / middle / bottom rows and left / center / right columns within the 3 × 3 kernel. These rows and columns are not channels.

Here blue = negative weight, red = positive weight, and pale = near zero. This color scale has a different meaning from the partner maps and is scaled separately for each kernel.

Exact weights, magnitude and mean
Magnitude (norm) is the square root of the sum of squared weights. Mean is the average of the nine weights. Use the numbers, not heatmap color intensity, to compare magnitudes across kernels.
See partners across the whole network
One map per convolution, in layer order. In each map, rows are output channels and columns are input channels. One cell is one kernel. Tap a cell to select it; scroll down to see later layers.


4. Inspect or export the matching partners
Each card or table row is one partner of your selected kernel, sorted by distance, closest first. Select a partner to inspect its own connections.


What does each table column mean?
Partner ID
The partner's unique kernel number in this snapshot.
Convolution
Its convolution index and name, such as 21: B11.conv1.
Block
The containing block, B01 to B20. Stem is before these blocks.
Role
Stem, conv1 or conv2: the convolution's position in the block.
Output channel / Input channel
The partner's row / column in its own convolution map, not in the selected kernel's layer.
Distance
Difference from the selected kernel under the chosen comparison. Smaller = closer; zero = identical under that comparison.
Same tight group?
Whether the partner and selected kernel belong to the same complete-linkage group. A No still means the pair directly matches.
Action
Select makes this partner the new query and refreshes the maps and table.

What is in the exported CSV?
One row per pair: query and partner IDs; the partner's layer, block, role and channel coordinates; comparison, cutoff and saved distance; raw Euclidean and relative differences; both magnitudes and means; and all nine original weights of each kernel in row order.
The CSV keeps numeric location codes for analysis: layer and channel indices start at 0; block 0 = stem; role 0 = stem, 1 = conv1, 2 = conv2. Raw relative difference is the raw Euclidean difference divided by the smaller magnitude. The export is this query's partner list, not all pairs in the network.
